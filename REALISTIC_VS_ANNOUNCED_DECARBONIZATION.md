# Realistic vs. Announced Decarbonization: Why the Global Picture Is Not a Recovery Pathway

[日本語版はこちら / Japanese version](REALISTIC_VS_ANNOUNCED_DECARBONIZATION_ja.md)

## Overview

A central gap in climate policy discourse is the difference between **Announced Decarbonization** — what nations commit to — and **Realistic Global Decarbonization** — what is likely to occur when all nations' trajectories are aggregated.

This distinction matters because:

- Announced Decarbonization frames climate progress optimistically, based on stated targets
- Realistic Global Decarbonization reflects the structural reality of a world where economic development and emission growth in the developing world offset reductions in the developed world
- The two scenarios lead to fundamentally different conclusions about whether current climate strategy constitutes a **recovery pathway**

This document examines both scenarios, their structural differences, and why Realistic Global Decarbonization — absent carbon fixation system restoration — cannot achieve atmospheric CO₂ stabilization.

All characterizations here are **conceptual and hypothetical frameworks**, not empirically validated trajectory predictions.

## 1. Scenario Definitions

### Scenario 2a: Announced Decarbonization Only

```text
All major emitting nations meet their stated NDCs (Nationally Determined Contributions)
on the announced timeline.

Developed economies achieve net-zero by 2050.
Developing economies follow agreed-upon transition pathways.
Global emissions peak and decline as announced.

This scenario does NOT include carbon fixation restoration.
Terrestrial ecosystems, soil carbon, and ocean biological systems continue on current trends.
```

This scenario is a **useful counterfactual** — it allows us to evaluate what happens even under optimistic policy assumptions, without sink restoration.

### Scenario 2b: Realistic Global Decarbonization Only

```text
Developed economies continue reducing emissions (as announced).
Developing economies continue expanding energy and industrial infrastructure.
The net global emission rate does not decline and may slightly increase.

This scenario reflects the structural reality of unequal development trajectories.

This scenario does NOT include carbon fixation restoration.
```

This scenario is a **realistic baseline** — it reflects what is structurally likely given current global economic and developmental dynamics.

Both scenarios exclude carbon fixation restoration.  
Both scenarios therefore represent what happens to atmospheric CO₂ and climate stability when the emission side is managed (or partially managed) but the sink side is not.

## 2. Announced Decarbonization: The Optimistic Counterfactual

In Scenario 2a, the best plausible outcome of current decarbonization policy is realized.

Even under this optimistic assumption, several structural problems persist:

### 2a-1. Atmospheric CO₂ Does Not Return to Pre-Industrial Levels

CO₂ already in the atmosphere does not disappear when emissions stop.  
CO₂ has a complex atmospheric lifetime — a portion remains for centuries to millennia.

Even with net-zero emissions achieved globally, atmospheric CO₂ would stabilize at current or near-current elevated levels, not decline to pre-industrial baselines.

Source reference:  
https://climate.nasa.gov/news/2915/the-atmosphere-getting-a-handle-on-carbon-dioxide/

### 2a-2. Temperature Does Not Immediately Stabilize

Due to thermal inertia (see [THERMAL_INERTIA_AND_TIME_LAG.md](./THERMAL_INERTIA_AND_TIME_LAG.md)):

- Ocean heat content continues to drive warming for decades after emissions peak
- Surface temperatures continue rising for 20–40 years even after emissions stabilize
- Full thermal equilibration takes centuries

### 2a-3. Carbon Sinks Continue to Be Stressed

During the warming decades following emission stabilization:

- Heat shocks from elevated baseline temperatures continue to damage terrestrial and ocean ecosystems
- Drought and wildfire cycles do not immediately stop
- Ocean stratification does not reverse without active intervention

### 2a-4. What 2a Achieves

```text
Scenario 2a achieves:
[+] Prevents CO₂ from rising to the highest possible levels
[+] Slows the rate of further warming
[+] Reduces the severity of some climate impacts
[+] Creates a slightly better foundation for carbon fixation restoration efforts

Scenario 2a does NOT achieve:
[-] Restoration of pre-industrial atmospheric CO₂
[-] Immediate temperature stabilization
[-] Recovery of damaged carbon fixation systems
[-] True planetary carbon balance
```

Scenario 2a is valuable and necessary, but it is a **ceiling of decarbonization-only outcomes**, not a recovery pathway.

## 3. Realistic Global Decarbonization: Net Emission Growth

Scenario 2b reflects the structural dynamics of global economic development.

### 3a. The Developed/Developing Emission Asymmetry

Developed economies (OECD countries, EU, North America, Japan, South Korea, Australia) are:

- Reducing emissions through renewable energy deployment
- Achieving some success in decoupling economic growth from emission growth
- Making measurable progress toward stated NDC targets

Developing economies (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, parts of Latin America) are:

- Rapidly expanding energy infrastructure to meet growing populations' needs
- Industrializing to achieve economic development comparable to developed nations
- Cannot be expected to forgo development on behalf of a problem primarily created by historical developed-world emissions

The structural consequence:

```text
Developed-world emission reductions
MINUS
Developing-world emission growth
= Net global change in emissions
```

In the current period, the developing-world growth component is large enough that:

```text
Net global decarbonization rate ≈ 0 or slightly negative
(i.e., global emissions are not declining — they may still be growing)
```

This is not a criticism of developing-world emissions.  
It reflects the structural reality of a world with 8 billion people, most of whom live in economies that are still expanding their energy and industrial base.

### 3b. Why This Is Not Framed as a Developing-World Problem

The responsibility framing is important:

- Historical cumulative emissions are primarily from developed nations
- Per-capita emissions in most developing nations remain far below developed-nation levels
- The structural problem is that a global energy and economic system still dependent on fossil fuels cannot transition equitably and rapidly without massive technology and finance transfer

Scenario 2b does not assign blame.  
It describes a structural outcome: when total global emissions are aggregated, the net trajectory is not one of decline.

### 3c. What 2b Achieves

```text
Scenario 2b achieves:
[+] Some reduction in developed-world emissions (real and valuable)
[+] Slows warming slightly relative to a pure BAU (business as usual) trajectory

Scenario 2b does NOT achieve:
[-] Net global emission reduction
[-] CO₂ stabilization
[-] Any of the outcomes listed under Scenario 2a
[-] A recovery pathway of any kind
```

In Scenario 2b, atmospheric CO₂ continues to rise.  
Temperature continues to rise.  
Carbon fixation systems continue to be stressed.

Scenario 2b is best described as:

```text
A trajectory that slows worsening relative to no action,
but does not constitute a pathway toward stability or recovery.
```

## 4. Why the Net Rate Matters More Than National-Level Progress

A common framing in climate communication emphasizes national or regional success stories:

- EU emissions have declined X% since 1990
- Country Y achieved net-zero on schedule
- Renewable energy now provides Z% of electricity in major economies

These achievements are real and significant.

However, the metric that matters for atmospheric CO₂ concentration is the **global net emission rate** — the aggregate of all sources and sinks across all nations.

If global net emissions are not declining, atmospheric CO₂ concentration will not stabilize, regardless of how impressive national-level progress is in some regions.

This is an accounting identity, not a political claim:

```text
Δ[Atmospheric CO₂] = Σ(Global emissions) − Σ(Global carbon sink uptake)

If Σ(Global emissions) ≥ Σ(Global carbon sink uptake):
→ [Atmospheric CO₂] does not decline
```

National-level success must be evaluated against global net trajectories, not only regional benchmarks.

## 5. The Implicit Assumption: Healthy Carbon Sinks

Both Scenario 2a and Scenario 2b implicitly assume, in the standard decarbonization model:

```text
"Reduced emissions → Atmospheric CO₂ stabilizes"
```

This logic holds **only if** carbon sinks are healthy enough to absorb emissions that remain after reduction.

The standard accounting for carbon neutrality:

```text
Carbon neutral ⟺ Emissions = Natural + technological carbon uptake
```

But if natural carbon uptake (terrestrial fixation, ocean uptake) is declining, the threshold for carbon neutrality becomes harder to reach.

The implicit assumption — that sinks remain functional — is **rarely made explicit** in public decarbonization narratives.

Making it explicit reveals the structural problem:

```text
Announced Decarbonization (2a):
"Emissions reduced → CO₂ stabilizes [assuming sinks remain intact]"

Realistic Decarbonization (2b):
"Emissions not reduced globally → CO₂ rises [regardless of sink state]"
```

In both cases, the scenario outcome depends critically on the state of carbon sinks.

In neither case does decarbonization alone restore degraded sinks.

## 6. Realistic Decarbonization + Degraded Sinks = Continued Accumulation

The worst-case structural combination:

```text
Net global emissions growing (Scenario 2b)
+
Carbon fixation systems degrading (terrestrial + ocean sinks in decline)
=
Accelerating atmospheric CO₂ accumulation
```

In this scenario:

- Emissions add CO₂ to the atmosphere
- Degraded sinks remove less CO₂ than they historically did
- The net accumulation rate is higher than emissions alone would predict

This is not a speculative extreme.  
Current trends show:
- Global forest loss continuing (net of plantation gains in some regions)
- Ocean phytoplankton productivity under stress in many regions
- Soil carbon loss from agricultural degradation ongoing
- Wetland loss continuing globally

The compounding of emission growth and sink loss is a structural risk, not a hypothetical one.

The specific rates, magnitudes, and timescales in the associated models are hypothetical.  
The structural logic — that combined emission growth and sink loss creates accelerating accumulation — follows directly from the carbon balance equation.

## 7. True Carbon Neutrality Requires Fixation Restoration

A critical conceptual clarification:

**Carbon neutrality**, as typically defined in policy contexts, means:

```text
Net CO₂ emissions = 0
(emissions matched by offsets, typically through afforestation, carbon credits, or CCS)
```

This is an accounting concept, not a physical recovery concept.

True physical carbon neutrality — where the Earth's carbon system returns to equilibrium — requires:

```text
Total CO₂ sources = Total CO₂ sinks (at pre-industrial equilibrium levels)
```

This is a much more demanding condition.

To achieve it:

1. Emissions must be reduced substantially
2. Carbon sink capacity must be restored to pre-degradation levels (or exceeded to draw down existing excess)
3. The thermal inertia problem must be addressed to allow sink recovery to occur

Without condition 2 and 3, accounting-level carbon neutrality does not represent physical recovery.

```text
"Emission reduction alone assumes healthy carbon sinks.
Without restoring those sinks, true carbon neutrality is structurally unachievable."
```

## 8. Structural Comparison of Scenarios

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Global emission trajectory | Rising | Declining (optimistic) | Flat/slightly rising | Declining |

The key observation: **no decarbonization-only scenario** — including the optimistic Scenario 2a — constitutes a recovery pathway in the absence of carbon fixation restoration.

Recovery requires addressing both sides of the carbon equation.

## 9. Conclusion

The distinction between Announced and Realistic Decarbonization reveals a critical strategic gap:

1. Even the optimistic Announced Decarbonization scenario does not constitute a recovery pathway — it is the ceiling of what decarbonization-only strategies can achieve
2. Realistic Global Decarbonization — reflecting actual structural dynamics — is not even a stabilization pathway, only a measure that slows the rate of worsening relative to no action

Both scenarios share the structural limitation that they address only the emission side of the carbon equation, leaving the fixation and absorption side unaddressed.

True atmospheric CO₂ stabilization — let alone recovery — requires:

```text
1. Emission reduction (decarbonization) — to reduce new CO₂ inputs
2. Carbon fixation restoration — to rebuild Earth's capacity to absorb and store CO₂
3. Thermal inertia management — to reduce the ongoing damage from accumulated ocean heat
```

Without all three, climate strategy remains structurally incomplete.

```text
"Decarbonization is a necessary first step.
It is not a sufficient path to planetary carbon recovery."
```

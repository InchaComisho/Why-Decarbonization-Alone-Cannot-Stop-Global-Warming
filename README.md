# Why Decarbonization Alone Cannot Stop Global Warming: The Missing Role of Carbon Fixation System Restoration

## Abstract

Decarbonization is necessary.

Reducing CO₂ emissions, shifting to renewable energy, improving energy efficiency, and transforming industrial systems are essential parts of climate action.

However, decarbonization alone is not enough to solve global warming and climate change.

The deeper problem is not only that human civilization emits too much CO₂.  
The deeper problem is that human civilization has also damaged the Earth’s natural ability to absorb, fix, store, and recycle carbon.

In other words, atmospheric CO₂ has increased not only because humans emitted it, but also because the Earth’s carbon fixation and carbon absorption systems have been weakened.

The conventional climate model is:

```text
Human activity
→ Increased CO₂ emissions
→ Global warming
→ Climate change
```

This model is not wrong.

But it is incomplete.

A deeper causal model is:

```text
Human activity
→ Destruction of soils, forests, oceans, wetlands, and microbial circulation
→ Weakening of carbon fixation and carbon absorption systems
→ CO₂ becomes harder to absorb, fix, store, and recycle
→ Atmospheric CO₂ accumulates
→ Global warming accelerates
→ Climate change intensifies
```

Therefore, the next stage of climate action must move:

```text
From decarbonization alone
to decarbonization + carbon fixation restoration.
```

## Core Thesis

Decarbonization is necessary, but it is not sufficient.

CO₂ emission reduction is only one side of the climate equation.

The other side is the restoration of carbon sinks, carbon fixation systems, soil carbon, microbial circulation, forests, wetlands, oceans, and natural water cycles.

The Earth is not only being overloaded with carbon.

```text
The Earth is losing its ability to process carbon.
```

Therefore, climate strategy must evolve from:

```text
Reduce CO₂ emissions.
```

to:

```text
Reduce CO₂ emissions
+
Restore the Earth’s ability to absorb, fix, store, and recycle CO₂.
```

## 1. What Is Decarbonization?

Decarbonization generally means reducing greenhouse gas emissions, especially CO₂ emissions, from human activities.

It includes:

```text
Renewable energy
Energy efficiency
Electrification
Electric vehicles
Reduction of fossil fuel use
Industrial transformation
Carbon pricing
Carbon credits
Carbon capture
Net-zero policies
Carbon neutrality
```

These measures are important.

They reduce the amount of CO₂ entering the atmosphere.

But decarbonization mainly focuses on the emission side of the problem.

It does not automatically restore damaged soils.  
It does not rebuild microbial ecosystems.  
It does not restore complex forests.  
It does not recover wetlands.  
It does not repair ocean ecosystems.  
It does not restart broken natural circulation systems.

This is the central limitation of decarbonization.

## 2. Why Decarbonization Is Necessary

CO₂ is a greenhouse gas.

When atmospheric CO₂ increases, it affects Earth’s radiative balance and contributes to global warming.

The IPCC states that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land.

Source:  
https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/resources/spm-headline-statements/

Therefore, reducing CO₂ emissions is necessary.

Without emission reduction, atmospheric CO₂ will continue to rise.

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 projects that fossil CO₂ emissions will reach a record high in 2025, showing that global decarbonization is still insufficient.

Source:  
https://globalcarbonbudget.org/fossil-fuel-co2-emissions-hit-record-high-in-2025/

This means decarbonization cannot be ignored.

But the important point is this:

```text
Necessary does not mean sufficient.
```

Decarbonization is required.

But decarbonization alone does not repair the broken carbon cycle.

## 3. The Missing Half of the Climate Equation

Most public climate discussions focus on emissions.

The common question is:

```text
How do we reduce CO₂ emissions?
```

This is important.

But it is only half of the equation.

The deeper question is:

```text
How do we restore the Earth’s ability to absorb, fix, store, and recycle CO₂?
```

Atmospheric CO₂ is affected by both emissions and sinks.

A simplified technical model is:

```text
Atmospheric CO₂ growth =
Human CO₂ emissions
- Land carbon uptake
- Ocean carbon uptake
- Long-term biological and geological carbon fixation
+ Carbon released by ecosystem degradation
```

This means atmospheric CO₂ can increase when:

```text
Human emissions increase
Carbon sinks weaken
Stored carbon is released
Soil carbon declines
Forests degrade
Wetlands disappear
Ocean ecosystems weaken
Microbial carbon processing declines
```

The mainstream decarbonization narrative emphasizes the first factor.

This document emphasizes the full equation.

## 4. CO₂ Is Both a Cause and a Symptom

CO₂ is a direct physical driver of warming.

This should not be denied.

However, CO₂ accumulation is also a symptom of a damaged planetary carbon cycle.

CO₂ increased because humans emitted it.

But CO₂ also continues to accumulate because the Earth’s natural absorption and fixation capacity has been weakened.

Therefore, CO₂ is:

```text
A direct cause of warming at the atmospheric level
+
A symptom of carbon-cycle failure at the planetary-system level
```

If we treat CO₂ only as an emission problem, the solution becomes narrow:

```text
Reduce emissions.
```

But if we understand CO₂ accumulation as a carbon-cycle failure, the solution becomes broader:

```text
Reduce emissions.
Restore carbon sinks.
Rebuild soil carbon.
Recover microbial circulation.
Regenerate forests.
Restore wetlands.
Support ocean ecosystems.
Repair natural water cycles.
```

This is the missing direction in many decarbonization discussions.

## 5. Carbon Neutrality Already Implies Absorption

Carbon neutrality is often explained as balancing greenhouse gas emissions with removals or absorption.

In principle, carbon neutrality is not only about reducing emissions.

It also requires maintaining and strengthening carbon absorption.

However, in public discussion, policy, and corporate communication, the emphasis often shifts toward:

```text
Renewable energy
Electric vehicles
Energy transition
Emission accounting
Carbon credits
Industrial reduction targets
```

These are important.

But they do not fully address the ecological foundation of carbon neutrality.

True carbon neutrality must include:

```text
Emission reduction
+
Carbon sink protection
+
Carbon fixation restoration
+
Ecosystem regeneration
```

Without the restoration of natural carbon sinks, carbon neutrality risks becoming an accounting framework rather than a planetary recovery strategy.

Carbon fixation systems are the natural mechanisms that absorb, store, transform, or recycle carbon within the Earth system.

They include:

```text
Forests
Soils
Soil organic matter
Humus
Wetlands
Peatlands
Grasslands
Agricultural soils
Oceans
Phytoplankton
Marine biological pumps
Coastal ecosystems
Mangroves
Seagrass beds
Soil microorganisms
Fungi
Root-zone microbial networks
Organic matter circulation
```

These systems are not passive scenery.

They are planetary infrastructure.

They regulate:

```text
Carbon
Water
Nutrients
Temperature
Biodiversity
Soil fertility
Food production
Ecosystem stability
Climate stability
```

The FAO states that soil organic carbon is crucial to soil health, fertility, and ecosystem services, and that soil holds three times as much carbon as the atmosphere.

Source:  
https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership/areas-of-work/soil-organic-carbon/en/

This means soil degradation is not only an agricultural issue.

It is a climate issue.

It is a carbon-cycle issue.

It is a civilization issue.

## 7. The Invisible Role of Microorganisms

Microorganisms are often missing from decarbonization debates.

Yet they are central to carbon fixation and soil carbon formation.

Soil microorganisms:

```text
Decompose organic matter
Transform nutrients
Support plant roots
Create soil structure
Influence humus formation
Contribute to soil organic carbon
Affect water retention
Regulate carbon release and carbon storage
```

Research shows that microbial necromass contributes significantly to soil organic carbon in croplands, grasslands, and forests.

Source:  
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0038071721002960

This means microorganisms are not merely decomposers.

They are carbon processors.

They determine whether carbon quickly returns to the atmosphere or becomes stabilized in soil.

When microbial ecosystems decline, the carbon cycle weakens.

The result is not only soil infertility.

The result is a weakened planetary carbon fixation system.

## 8. Why Decarbonization Alone Is Not Enough

Decarbonization alone is insufficient for five major reasons.

### 8.1 Degraded soils do not automatically recover

Reducing CO₂ emissions does not immediately restore soil organic carbon.

It does not automatically rebuild:

```text
Microbial networks
Humus
Root-zone ecosystems
Soil structure
Water retention
Nutrient circulation
Carbon storage capacity
```

Soil regeneration requires active restoration.

### 8.2 Forests are not restored by planting trees alone

A forest is not merely a collection of trees.

A real forest is a complex ecosystem involving:

```text
Trees
Understory plants
Fungi
Soil microorganisms
Insects
Animals
Water cycles
Soil carbon
Biodiversity
```

Monoculture tree planting cannot fully replace a mature, diverse forest ecosystem.

If forest restoration focuses only on counting planted trees, it may fail to restore real carbon fixation capacity.

### 8.3 Ocean carbon sinks are difficult to see

Oceans are major carbon sinks.

They absorb large amounts of human-caused CO₂ and heat.

However, ocean warming, acidification, pollution, nutrient disruption, and ecosystem decline can weaken marine carbon absorption.

The Global Carbon Budget tracks the role of land and ocean sinks in absorbing human-caused CO₂ emissions.

Source:  
https://globalcarbonbudget.org/gcb-2025/the-global-carbon-budget-faqs-2025/

If ocean carbon sinks weaken, more CO₂ remains in the atmosphere.

Therefore, decarbonization must be connected to ocean restoration.

### 8.4 Organic matter is being removed from natural circulation

Leaves, branches, food waste, crop residues, and other organic materials should return to soil and microbial circulation.

Instead, modern society often burns, discards, or removes organic matter from local ecosystems.

This breaks the organic carbon cycle.

Organic matter should be seen not as waste, but as a raw material for soil carbon, microbial life, and humus formation.

Decarbonization must include organic matter recycling.

### 8.5 Climate change weakens carbon sinks

Warming itself damages carbon sinks.

It increases:

```text
Drought
Wildfires
Forest stress
Soil degradation
Ocean stress
Ecosystem collapse
Agricultural instability
```

These effects weaken the Earth’s ability to absorb and store carbon.

This creates a feedback loop:

```text
CO₂ accumulation
→ Global warming
→ Drought, fires, ocean stress, soil degradation
→ Weaker carbon sinks
→ More CO₂ remains in the atmosphere
→ Further warming
```

This feedback cannot be solved by emission reduction alone.

It requires carbon sink restoration.

## 9. The Broken Reservoir Analogy

Decarbonization is like reducing the inflow into a damaged reservoir.

If the reservoir is healthy, reducing inflow may be enough.

But if the reservoir is cracked, leaking, and structurally damaged, reducing inflow is not enough.

The reservoir itself must be repaired.

In the climate system:

```text
The inflow = human CO₂ emissions
The reservoir = soils, forests, oceans, wetlands, and carbon sinks
The leakage = carbon released from degraded ecosystems
The repair = carbon fixation system restoration
```

Therefore, climate action must include both:

```text
Reducing the inflow
+
Repairing the reservoir
```

This is why decarbonization alone cannot solve the climate crisis.

## 10. The Limit of Emission-Centered Thinking

Emission-centered thinking reduces climate action to a single metric:

```text
How much CO₂ did humans emit?
```

But the Earth system is more complex.

A full climate strategy must also ask:

```text
How much carbon can soils store?
How healthy are microbial ecosystems?
How resilient are forests?
How stable are ocean sinks?
How much organic matter returns to soil?
How much carbon is released by fires and degradation?
How much water-cycle disruption reduces ecosystem productivity?
How much biodiversity loss weakens carbon fixation?
```

Without these questions, decarbonization remains incomplete.

The problem is not only carbon emission.

The problem is carbon circulation failure.

The next climate strategy should be built on two pillars:

```text
1. Decarbonization
2. Carbon fixation restoration
```

Decarbonization reduces the pressure.

Carbon fixation restoration rebuilds the Earth’s processing capacity.

Both are necessary.

The strategic shift is:

```text
From CO₂ reduction alone
to CO₂ reduction + carbon processing recovery.
```

This is not anti-decarbonization.

It is a higher-level extension of decarbonization.

But if the deeper cause is carbon-cycle collapse and natural circulation failure, then symptom control is not enough.

The goal should not be to dominate the Earth system.

The goal should be to restore the Earth system’s natural ability to regulate itself.

This document therefore distinguishes between:

```text
Artificial control of climate symptoms
```

and:

```text
Restoration of natural carbon and water circulation
```

## 14. A Technical Interpretation of Decarbonization Limits

From a systems perspective, decarbonization reduces only one major input variable:

```text
Human industrial CO₂ emissions
```

But atmospheric CO₂ concentration depends on multiple variables:

```text
Industrial emissions
Land-use emissions
Ocean carbon uptake
Land carbon uptake
Soil organic carbon storage
Wetland and peat carbon storage
Forest productivity
Microbial carbon stabilization
Biological carbon pump efficiency
Carbon released by fires
Carbon released by soil degradation
Carbon released by ecosystem collapse
```

A simplified equation is:

```text
Atmospheric CO₂ increase =
Industrial emissions
+ Land-use and ecosystem degradation emissions
- Ocean carbon uptake
- Land carbon uptake
- Soil and biological carbon fixation
```

Decarbonization targets the first term.

Carbon fixation restoration strengthens the negative terms and reduces ecosystem degradation emissions.

Therefore:

```text
Decarbonization reduces carbon input.
Carbon fixation restoration increases carbon processing capacity.
```

Both are required.
